
    Experimentation Guidelines
    Statistical Significance
    1. Why Two Samples? (And Why Not a Z-test?)

    When we run experiments (like tuning genetic algorithms), we usually have a Baseline (Control) and a
    Treatment (the change we made).

    We want to know if the Treatment is actually better, or if the difference we see is just random noise.

            The Problem: We do not know the "true" population mean or variance. We only have estimates from
            our samples.
            The Solution: We compare the means of two samples to infer if their underlying populations are
            different.

    Why not a Student's t-test? The classic Student's t-test assumes that the two populations we are comparing
    have the same variance (homogeneity of variance), even if their means differ. In real-world experiment design
    —especially when testing changes to algorithms—this assumption is often violated. Our "change" might make
    the results more volatile (higher variance) or more stable (lower variance).

    Why Welch's t-test? Welch’s t-test (the unequal variance t-test) is the modern standard. It does not assume
    equal variances. It is generally safer and performs better than Student's t-test when sample sizes or variances
    are unequal.

    The seed models need to be turned into samples because our 1 million file quota on PACE-ICE prevents us
    from training our evolved models with the entire training dataset. This may seem like it would harm our
    models' performance, which is the right intuition for the wrong setting. Indeed, if our models outperformed
    the paper's results under adverse conditions, then we could reliably compare them to the paper's results.
    However, deep learning models tend to suffer from performance degradation when fed many similar training
    examples.

    Imagine you've never seen a cat, so you decide to look at pictures of them. Procedure 1 dictates that you see
    120 pictures, 60 of which are unique cats, and the other 60 are not cats. Alternatively, Procedure 2 shows you
    120,000 pictures, 90,000 of which are absolutely not cats, and 29,880 of which are repeated pictures of the
    same 120 unique cats. Yes, you've seen twice as many unique cats under Procedure 2, but if you see a new cat
    that doesn't look anything like the 120, you will probably not consider it a cat. This isn't necessarily an artifact
    of the model parameters or architecture causing overfitting. The dataset itself induces the overfitting by being
    too redundant and encouraging memorization by construction.

    There are an obscene number of frames in the original training dataset, and by sampling every fifth frame, we
    are reducing the amount of redundancy that would be fed into the models.
